The truth behind "one short prompt" games

Social media is full of games "made with one prompt". Some of these claims are real and well documented; most leave out the harness, the follow-up turns, the expertise of the person driving, or the fact that the game is not fun. This page classifies the public examples and shows what a short prompt really hides.

Short answer

Yes: a current coding agent can turn one short prompt into a playable small web game, and the best-documented example proves it. No: none of the documented cases produced a game that the author or an independent reviewer called good to play. The prompt gets you a first playable; design, feel and polish still come from people. What the creators themselves say

  • Andrej Karpathy, who coined "vibe coding" on 2025-02-02, scoped it to "throwaway weekend projects". Verified
  • Simon Willison, after several agent-built games: "They've all been deeply disappointing from a gameplay perspective — it turns out designing games that are fun remains a uniquely human trait." Verified
  • Benchmarks agree from a different angle: agents solve about half of realistic game-dev tasks and find about half of real bugs (page 10). Verified

Why the first playable is easy and the good game is hard

Easy for a model

Common patterns with millions of examples: a game loop, Three.js scene setup, WASD movement, a score counter, a title screen, procedural shapes and particle effects.

Hard for a model

Feel and tuning (needs playing), difficulty curves, level design, readable visual style across assets, "is this fun?", bugs that only appear on real phones.

So the short prompt…

…reliably produces the easy column and a plausible-looking default for the hard column. Your work is the hard column.

Inference from the examples above and the GameDevBench split (gameplay tasks 51.4% vs 2D-graphics tasks 33.0%).

Getting the most out of a short prompt

  1. Write a spec, not a wish. The vendor demos that look best (Gemini 3, the Codex racer) used long prompts or skills. Name the genre, controls, win and lose rules, art strategy, target devices and stack.
  2. Give the agent eyes. Ask for the two test hooks and Playwright screenshots on desktop and phone sizes (page 10).
  3. Tell it to work independently and commit often, as Willison did, so you can roll back.
  4. Play the result, then send specific notes: "jump feels floaty; enemies too fast in level 2", not "make it better".
  5. Set a budget. Autonomous loops can burn millions of tokens; check your plan's limits before leaving an agent running.
