Publishing and legal

Where to put a finished game, what it costs, what Windows does to unsigned programs, how to handle asset licences, what the law says about AI-generated content, and how to make a "Mario-like" game without a letter from Nintendo's lawyers.

Not legal advice

This page summarises public sources as of 2026-10-17 for orientation. Law and store rules change quickly and differ by country. For anything you sell, check the current terms and, if money is involved, ask a lawyer or tax adviser.

Shipping a Windows program

  • SmartScreen will warn your players. A new or unsigned .exe shows a "Windows protected your PC" warning until it builds reputation. Since 2024, EV certificates no longer skip this. Windows 11 Smart App Control can block unsigned programs outright. Verified (Microsoft Learn)
  • Signing options: Azure Artifact Signing (formerly Trusted Signing) costs about $9.99 per month, but individuals qualify only in the USA and Canada; organisations in the EU and UK qualify with 3+ years of tax history. For an EU individual the fallback is an OV certificate at roughly $150–500 per year. Verified
  • Practical route for a hobbyist in the EU: ship through itch.io, Steam or the Microsoft Store and avoid buying a certificate; or ship unsigned and tell players to click "More info → Run anyway". Practice
  • Wrapping a web game: Electron is the common choice for Steam (large but proven); Tauri produces much smaller apps. Godot and Unity export Windows builds directly. Practice
  • Engine notices: Godot is MIT-licensed, so you must include its licence text; Unity Personal is free up to $200,000 revenue plus funding in 12 months, and the Runtime Fee was cancelled in 2024. Verified

Who owns AI-generated content?

WherePosition as of 2026-10-17Evidence
USAThe Copyright Office report of 2025-01-29: purely AI-generated material is not protected, and "prompts do not alone provide sufficient control". Human selection, arrangement and edits can be protected. Thaler v. Perlmutter: Supreme Court declined the case on 2026-03-02, so human authorship stays required. Zarya of the Dawn: the text and arrangement kept protection, the Midjourney images did not.Verified

UKThe law (s.9(3)) still gives computer-generated works a 50-year right, but the government proposed removing it in its 2026-03-18 report. Getty v. Stability (2025-11-04): the model weights are not an infringing copy; appeal permitted.Verified

What this means for an agent-built game

Your game as a whole has protectable human parts: design, levels you author, text you write, selection and arrangement, your edits. Raw AI sprites, music and models are probably not yours to enforce, so others can copy them. You can still be liable if an output reproduces someone else's protected work. Keep a record of your human contribution (commit history, design notes, edits). Inference from the sources above.

In practice: AI-written code needs no disclosure on Steam; AI sprites, music, voices, text or store art do; an LLM-driven NPC is live-generated and needs guardrails and, in the EU, usually a notice. Practice

Germany: a website offering your game that is not purely private needs an Impressum (§5 DDG, §18 MStV), even without profit intent. Verified (state media authorities)

Clone, trademark and fan-game risk

Free to use

Mechanics and rules: running, jumping, stomping, coins, falling blocks, match-3. Tetris v. Xio (2012): "Tetris Holding has given the rules of its game to the public domain, but has kept the rights to its expression."

Protected

Expression: characters, art, names, music, sound effects, specific level layouts, distinctive look and feel. Spry Fox v. LOLApps (2012): copying a game's object hierarchy, theme and overall expression was enough for the claim to proceed; it settled with the clone's IP handed over.

Also possible

Patents on specific mechanics. Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued Pocketpair over Palworld in 2024 on patents, not copyright; the case is pending.

Nintendo's track record: DMCA takedowns of fan games that used its characters (AM2R and Pokémon Uranium in 2016, more than 500 fan games on Game Jolt in 2016), takedowns of the Super Mario 64 PC port binaries in 2020, and a $2.4M settlement with the Yuzu emulator makers in 2024. Verified (court documents and press)

Clone-safe checklist

  1. Original hero, enemies, world, names, UI, music and levels. No ripped sprites, sounds, fonts or level layouts.
  2. Never prompt an AI model with franchise names ("Mario-style plumber") or living artists' names; reject outputs that look familiar; reverse-image-search key art. The Disney/Universal v. Midjourney case is about outputs that depict studio characters.
  3. Coin a distinctive title and search it in TMview and the USPTO database before you buy a domain or make a store page.
  4. Do not market your game as "X-like" using someone else's trademark.
  5. Fan games: assume a takedown, never monetise, never publish files containing the original's assets.

Release checklist

  • CREDITS.md complete; credits visible in the game and on the store page; MIT and OFL notices included.
  • AI-asset log: tool, plan, date, prompt, licence terms for each generated asset.
  • Store AI disclosure filled in honestly (Steam survey, itch.io field).
  • Title cleared in trademark databases; no franchise names or look-alike logos.
  • Runtime AI (if any): moderation, report button, visible AI notice.
  • Money: itch.io tax interview; Steam fee and wait; Impressum on your own site; ask a tax adviser before real income.

Not covered here: GDPR and privacy for analytics, age ratings, EU consumer and VAT rules, and China's game licensing regime.
