Five directions for our next discussion

This research answers "how do AI agents build games today?". Each direction below is a follow-up worth a session of its own: what we would decide, what we would build or measure, and what is still unknown.

1 · Build starter project 2 or 4 together

Why: the fastest way to calibrate everything on this site is to watch an agent build a game and measure it.

We would: run one prompt from page 14 in Cursor, log time, tokens, follow-up count and bugs found by tests versus by playing.

Unknown today: no published study measures agent-assisted game time for beginners; our own numbers would replace the estimates on pages 8 and 9.

2 · A reusable "game agent kit" for our machines

Why: the strongest one-prompt results used skills and templates (page 11).

We would: assemble a Cursor rules and skills folder: stack choice, the two test hooks, Playwright smoke tests, CREDITS.md rules, a licence guard; compare against awesome-gamedev-agent-skills and game-creator.

Unknown today: how much a kit improves results versus a bare prompt; nobody has published a controlled comparison.

3 · A consistent AI art pipeline, tested

Why: art consistency is the biggest gap between "playable" and "looks like a game" (pages 2 and 3).

We would: generate one character with idle, run and jump animations using two routes (a pixel-native tool and a general image model plus cleanup script), and score frame drift, palette, grid alignment, cost and time.

Unknown today: current tools change monthly; vendor demos are not independent evidence.

4 · Agent playtesting beyond smoke tests

Why: benchmarks show agents find about half of real bugs (page 10).

We would: seed ten known bugs into a small game and compare a state-hook bot, a vision-based Playwright agent and a human tester.

Unknown today: whether newer models close the GBQA and GameDevBench gaps; those papers used early-2026 models.

5 · Runtime AI in games, and the rules around it

Why: everything here is AI at build time. LLM-driven characters and world models (page 6) are a different product with different obligations.

We would: prototype one LLM-driven NPC with guardrails; review Steam's live-generated rules, EU AI Act Article 50 and per-player cost.

Unknown today: the EU Court of Justice opinion in Like Company v. Google (reported for 2026-11-03), and how regulators treat monetised hobby projects.

Open questions this research could not settle

  • Whether OpenAI's develop-web-game skill will return to the main branch, or has been moved or retired.
  • Independent quality reviews of the vendor-run one-prompt games (Codex racer, Fable 5 demos).
  • The real entry count and enforcement method of Vibe Jam 2026.
  • CrazyGames' base revenue share; Steam's 21- versus 30-day wait (conflicting official pages).
  • Whether AI-written source code (as opposed to images or text) is treated differently for copyright; no source addressed it directly.
  • How fast world models (Genie, Matrix-Game and similar) become usable for shipped games rather than demos.

Full list of gaps and conflicts on the sources page.
